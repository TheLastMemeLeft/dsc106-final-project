Human Consequence

The first thing people notice is the rain shifting. The emissions we choose now decide how far it shifts, and shape the whole climate, from the clouds to the deep ocean. Scroll down to trace it to its cause and to what keeps going long after CO₂ stops rising, following two futures: one with low emissions and one with high emissions.

Amy Trinh, Minh Pham, Aryan Agarwal · DSC 106 Final Project

Where the rain is heading

Rain is the change people notice first. Some regions get wetter and others get drier. With low emissions it barely changes after about 2060: the average shift goes from +3.4% to +3.8% between 2060–2064 and 2095–2099. With high emissions it keeps growing. "Area changed by more than 10%" goes from 35% to 59% over the same periods. What drives it starts on land.

The map shows the change in rainfall compared to 1995-2014, averaged over 8 models. Move the slider through time and switch between low and high emissions. Click the map, then scroll to zoom.

Data: CMIP6 precipitation, 8 models, 5-year averages, 4° grid. Grey areas are deserts (under 0.25 mm of rain a day), where a % change isn't meaningful.

What we put into the air

The rain shift starts here, with what we put into the air. Human emissions pushed CO₂ in the air from 313 ppm in 1950 to 391 ppm in 2014. After 2014 the path depends on what we choose. With low emissions, CO₂ peaks around 2061 and then starts to fall. With high emissions, it reaches 1,102 ppm by 2100.

Data: GFDL-ESM4 co2mass, converted to ppm. Scenarios: SSP1-2.6 (low), SSP5-8.5 (high).

More CO₂, warmer oceans

As CO₂ climbs, the ocean surface warms with it. In the model the two rise almost in step. By 2100 the sea surface averages 18.8 °C with low emissions and 20.7 °C with high emissions.

Each dot is one year. Hover over a dot to see the year.

Data: GFDL-ESM4 (one model, the same one as the CO₂ chart), global area-weighted mean sea surface temperature. The scenarios also differ in other gases and aerosols, so this shows CO₂ and warming rising together, not CO₂ alone.

Sea surface temperature, 1950-2100

The warming isn't spread evenly, and almost no part of the ocean escapes it. With low emissions the surface levels off after the 2061 CO₂ peak, as rain does (10-year averages: 18.8 °C around 2061, 18.7 °C in 2091–2100). With high emissions it keeps climbing, to 20.7 °C in 2100. Press play to watch it spread. Switch to "Change" to see how much each area has warmed since 1950-1979.

Data: GFDL-ESM4 tos (sea surface temperature), yearly mean, 2° grid. Years after 2014 are projections.

How much of the ocean has warmed

By 2100 the average patch of ocean has warmed by 0.9 °C with low emissions and by 2.8 °C with high emissions, compared with 1950-1979. The whole distribution moves, not just a few hot spots.

The bars show the share of ocean area by how much it has warmed. They follow the year and scenario picked on the map. The dashed line is 1980.

Where the heat goes

The surface is only the start. The ocean keeps soaking up heat and carrying it down. With low emissions, the surface stops warming after CO₂ peaks around 2061. Yet the water at 1,000 m keeps warming: from +0.2 °C in 2061 to +0.45 °C in 2100. Rain and the surface settled after 2061, but the water at depth didn't. That sinking heat is what keeps sea level rising.

Each line shows how much the ocean has warmed at each depth since 1950-1979. Press play or drag the slider to watch the heat sink.

Switch to "Over time" to line this up with the other charts. After 2061 the surface line goes flat, but the heat stored in the top 2,000 m keeps climbing, the same shape as sea level in the next chart.

Data: GFDL-ESM4 thetao (sea water potential temperature, the same model as the sea surface map), global area-weighted mean at each depth, yearly mean.

Sea level rise from warming water

Water expands as it warms, so sea level rises even without any melting ice. But the ocean is slow. With low emissions, CO₂ peaks around 2061 and rain and the sea surface level off, yet sea level keeps climbing. It goes from +10.6 cm in 2061 to +16.4 cm in 2100. Heat that's already in the ocean keeps sinking deeper and expanding the water.

Sea level follows the heat stored in the top 2,000 m from the previous chart almost exactly (correlation 0.999, every year, both futures). It doesn't follow CO₂ or the surface: after 2061 with low emissions, CO₂ falls and the surface stays flat, while stored heat and sea level keep rising together.

The shaded band is the range across models.

Data: CMIP6 zostoga (global thermosteric sea level: expansion from heat only, not added water from rain or ice), averaged over 4 models (ACCESS-CM2, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-LR), relative to 1995-2014. These are different models from the GFDL-ESM4 run used for CO₂ and temperature.

So the timing matters

Every chart on this page splits at the same point. Some effects settle soon after CO₂ stops rising: rain and the sea surface. Others keep going: deep heat and sea level. By 2100 the two futures are 655 ppm of CO₂, 1.9 °C of sea surface temperature and 16.3 cm of sea level apart. By 2095–2099, 59% of the map's area has a rain change over 10% with high emissions, against 18% with low. And because the deep ocean keeps heating after emissions fall, every decade we wait adds sea level rise we can't take back. Cutting emissions sooner gives the smaller number. Waiting gives the bigger one.

Write-up

So far, we have decided on what topic we want to focus on for our final project and completed a working prototype of what the webpage will look like. We aim to tell a story about human consequences on Earth's environment, ranging from the air to the ocean. Our project will include multiple different visualizations that use data regarding precipitation, CO2 emissions, sea surface temperatures, and sea levels to show the impacts of climate change. These visualizations include interactive heatmaps, line charts, scatterplots, and histograms that can be filtered and changed using sliders and tooltips. We plan to continue to improve their design until the final deadline.

The most challenging part of the project is trying to convey the message in a compelling and cohesive way. We want to be able to clearly show the readers a story about the data while also making the entire process interesting and exciting. To do this, we attempted to make our webpage change environments from the sky to ocean as the viewers scroll down, emphasized by the background colors and other minor details like a depth meter on the left of the screen. We hope that this will allow readers to understand the impacts of the data and make connections between the graphs while still making it a pleasant viewing experience.
